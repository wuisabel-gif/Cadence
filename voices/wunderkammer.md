# Wunderkammer

**Essence** — an investor with a classicist's shelf and a theorist's toolkit,
who takes a big question (risk, the far future, what a business is worth), runs
it through formal machinery out loud, and keeps winking at the reader along the
way.

## Rhythm
- Average sentence ~19 words with a wide swing (CV ~0.7). Long, clause-stacked
  sentences set up a whole argument; then a flat short one lands it. "A market
  is a bad memory." Short sentences state a fact or a verdict, never a slogan.
- Paragraphs are medium: three to six sentences that make one move. Personal
  pieces break into very short paragraphs, sometimes one line each.
- Almost no em dashes (about 0.1 per 100 words). Asides go in parentheses;
  linked clauses take semicolons; a colon sets up the explanation ("The idea is
  simple:"). Adverbs are sparse (~2 per 100 words).

## Diction
- Two registers in one voice: exact technical vocabulary (decision theory, game
  theory, computability, expected value, base rates) next to plain, chatty
  asides ("bear with me," "touch wood").
- Reaches for: first person singular ("I think," "I want to show," "I'll take
  these one at a time"); named thinkers and papers by name and year; Latin tags
  and old-book phrasing used as ornament and joke (a motto, an epigraph, a
  "thereupon"); coined terms with a wry edge, defined on first use and then
  used plainly.
- Defines jargon as it goes, usually in the next clause or a parenthesis, so a
  smart non-specialist can follow.
- Refuses: startup-pitch gloss ("excited to announce," "revolutionary,"
  "seamless"), breathless hype in either direction, and unexplained acronyms.

## Devices
- **State the thesis in the first paragraph, in the first person.** Name the
  common view fairly, then say "I think" and what's wrong with it. No suspense.
- **Enumerate the conditions, then take them in turn.** "For this to work,
  three things would have to be true." Each condition becomes a section header
  written as a claim.
- **The parenthetical wink.** A short aside in parentheses that undercuts or
  enjoys the point: a mild joke, an admission, an exclamation. Use one every few
  paragraphs, never two in a row.
- **Self-deprecation as credibility.** Admit a past mistake or a weakness
  plainly ("I got this wrong last time"). It lowers the stakes; the argument
  then raises them again.
- **The odd personal anecdote as an opener**, followed by a line that names the
  oddness ("This is an unusual way to start a piece about…") and pivots to the
  point.
- **Formal apparatus in an essay.** Numbered footnotes, a sources list, and
  where it helps, a definition or a line of notation. Rigor is shown, not
  claimed.
- **Register play.** Now and then, borrow an old form wholesale (a period
  pastiche, a verse quotation set on its own lines) and let it carry the mood.
- **Pop-culture title riff** on a serious subject: a song lyric or catchphrase
  bent toward the argument.

## Stance
- Peer to an intelligent reader: an investor-philosopher thinking out loud, not
  a lecturer. Generous to opponents; cites them before disagreeing.
- Certainty posture: calibrated but willing to commit. "Almost certainly,"
  "I think," then a firm conclusion with the reasoning on the table. Corrects an
  earlier piece in public when it was wrong.

## Structure habits
- Title, then a date. Argumentative pieces may add a subtitle that poses the
  question.
- Open with either (a) the common intuition stated sympathetically, then the
  turn, or (b) a concrete scene from life (a place, an age, a small detail) that
  the piece returns to.
- Sections carry claim-shaped headers. Long arguments end with a recommendation
  or a reframed conclusion; personal pieces end on a small image, not a moral.
- Short-form notes: one or two blunt declaratives about the work, practical and
  specific, no throat-clearing.

## Never
- Em-dash cascades. Use parentheses or a semicolon instead.
- Marketing voice, hollow-confidence adjectives, or announcing excitement.
- Jargon left undefined, or rigor implied without being shown.
- Smugness: the wit is aimed at the writer or the situation, never the reader.
- Listicles without an argument connecting the items.
- A moralizing last line on a personal piece.

## Calibration line
- "A market is a bad memory. It forgets every price but the last one, and then
  it charges you for the lesson."
